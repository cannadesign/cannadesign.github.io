Compass: from one-off screens to a system three teams could build on

Before Compass, every screen at Kruzer was rebuilt from zero and nothing quite agreed with anything else. Building the system, and the semantic token scale underneath it, cut the cycle time on a design ticket from 4.2 to 2.5 days, measured in Jira.

Compass is the design system I built from scratch at Kruzer, the platform behind Fast Shop's fulfillment operation. It started as a way to stop rebuilding the same status pill for the third time, and ended up as the layer that let three products stay coherent while the platform scaled to 80+ stores.

01 · Why it was hard

Before: everything took longer than it should

There was no shared foundation. Every new screen started from an empty frame, so the same components were redrawn over and over with small differences nobody had decided on: the primary button was one shade of blue in the queue and another in the order detail. Design took longer because nothing could be reused, engineering took longer because every handoff described a slightly new component, and review cycles took longer because most comments were about inconsistencies, not whether the solution was right.

“Consistency” is not a business case, and no one funds tidiness, so the pointer I argued for was delivery speed: how long a change takes to get from decision to production, across three products on the same platform. That framing can be proven wrong — if cycle time didn’t move, Compass was an expensive preference — and tying it to a number engineering already tracked meant I was accountable to their measurement, not my own.

A design system that designers love and engineers route around is a failed one, so the founding conversation was with the people who would consume it: who owns a component once it exists, what a handoff needs to contain to stop being re-negotiated, and who is allowed to say no to a new variant. Settling those before the library had anything in it was the only moment that conversation was cheap.

Most of the review time was spent arguing about padding, not about whether the design solved the problem.

“Consistency” is not a business case. Delivery speed is, and it can be proven wrong.

02 · Decision: only the semantic layer ships
1

Only the semantic layer is allowed in a design file

Problem

Naming tokens after what they look like — blue-500 — tells you nothing about when to use them. People guess, and the guessing is where inconsistency comes back in.

Decision

Split three layers. Primitive: raw values like blue-500, space-4, referenced but never designed with directly. Semantic: what a value means — action-primary, status-success — the only layer a designer or engineer touches. Component: rare, purpose-built overrides.

Why

Asking “what breaks if we rebrand, or add dark mode” early is what forced the token layer to exist before the components. A system that can’t survive a colour change was never a system.

Result

Changing the meaning of “success” across three products became one edit. Adding a density mode became a token set, not a redesign. A new designer can read a file and understand why a colour is there.

03 · Decision: governance is part of the deliverable
2

A library without governance is just a folder

Problem

The part teams skip is the part that decides whether the system is alive in a year. A component nobody owns rots; a library with no rule against “just one more variant” drifts back into a folder.

Decision

Ship rules with the files, not after them: a new component has to prove it appears in at least two products, a change to a semantic token goes through review because it moves everything downstream, and anything deprecated gets a removal date instead of sitting there forever. Documentation counts as part of the component — a “use this when” that doesn’t exist is a component that will be misused.

Why

I asked engineers what they were actually missing when a spec arrived, and the answers shaped the documentation more than any design-system article did. Deciding who can say no, and on what grounds, is what keeps a library from drifting back into a folder.

Result

Componentisation is where the rules stop being a document and start being enforced. A button is one component with variants for hierarchy, size, state and icon, spaced by token, not by hand: an engineer opening it receives a rule, not a rectangle.

Governance isn’t bureaucracy. It’s the answer to “can I just add one more variant?”

04 · What shipped

The user here is the team, and the job is confidence

Compass has no end user. Its users are designers and engineers, and what they're hiring it for isn't beauty — it's the ability to make a decision once and not argue it again. Framed that way, the measure of success stops being how many components exist and becomes how few decisions each screen requires.

The job isn’t consistency. It’s making a decision once and never having to defend it again.

A live slice of Compass. Every component here reads from the same three tokens, so changing one keeps the whole set coherent. That is the entire argument for a design system, in about four seconds.

A library that has never survived a real product is a mood board. Pick & Pack was migrated screen by screen while it was still being built, so every gap showed up under deadline pressure, not in a workshop. Some components didn’t survive contact; others revealed the semantic layer was missing a meaning rather than a value, which is the failure worth catching — a component override is a patch, a missing semantic token is a flaw in the system itself. I sat in engineering’s implementation reviews through the migration, because a token that exists in Figma and not in code is a token that doesn’t exist.

A token that exists in Figma and not in code is a token that doesn’t exist.

Migration is also where the atom-and-molecule distinction stops being vocabulary. A select is not one component; it is a field, a label, a tooltip, a chip, a list and an error state composed together. Getting the composition right is what made a new select variant cost minutes instead of an afternoon, and it is the difference between a library and a folder of screens.

05 · Impact

What it changed

There's no usability test for a design system, and no funnel — the evidence came from three places instead. The audit gave a hard before: around twenty blues, seven paddings and four button heights live in production, counted rather than estimated. Pick & Pack was the first consumer, proving the system against a real product before anyone else adopted it. And Jira, which the team already used to track demand, gave the after: the same class of work, measured before and after adoption. A metric I invent to prove my own project is worth roughly nothing in a review; a metric engineering already trusts is much harder to wave away.

The clearest signal came from the tracker, not from opinion: once teams were building from Compass instead of from scratch, Jira throughput on design demands rose sharply, and review comments shifted from spacing and colour to whether the flow was right. What that number can't prove, and I'd say so before anyone asked: ticket throughput is confounded — the team also grew and the sprint cadence changed once — so the 40% is the delta engineering leads read from their own board over the same class of work, the best number available, not a controlled one. The review-comment shift is the softer signal, and honestly the one I trust more, because nobody can game what a colleague chooses to argue about.

How it was measured: both numbers come from the Jira board engineering already used to track demand, comparing the same class of design ticket before and after adoption — 40% on throughput, 4.2 days down to 2.5 on cycle time. Neither is a controlled experiment, and both are readings I recall rather than a report I can still pull, so treat the magnitudes as approximate and the direction as the claim. The measurement had to belong to someone other than me, or it would have been marketing; the confounds above are named on purpose, not buried.

06 · What I'd do differently

What I’d do differently

I would write the governance rules before the component library, not after it. Adoption took about two months to reach all three products — faster than expected, which meant the rules arrived while people were already adding variants, so the first months of governance were spent undoing things instead of preventing them. I would also have led with a different number: throughput was the easy metric to reach for, and cycle time — roughly 4.2 days down to 2.5 — is the harder one to argue with, but I only went looking for it after 40% was already the headline.

Reach for the number that could embarrass you first.

What I'd defend in a design review

The decision I would defend hardest is refusing to let the component layer grow. Every one-off override was a signal that the semantic layer had a gap, and fixing the gap is slower in the moment and cheaper for everyone after. I also count governance as part of the deliverable: a system nobody owns drifts back to where it started.

The argument I had to win was with engineering, not design, because a system is adopted by people who didn't ask for it, and this one was about taking something away: only the semantic layer would be published, meaning giving up a naming convention they were comfortable with. What made it land was the audit — twenty near-identical blues live in production is not an opinion, and it moved the conversation from taste to cost in one meeting.
