Pick & Pack: one order, three people, one screen each

Fast Shop sells two-hour delivery. The tool behind that promise ran on phone calls and spreadsheets. This is how I designed the platform that replaced them, for the store, the distribution centre and the driver at once, and why the exceptions got designed before the happy path.

01 · The promise on the checkout page

Two hours, promised before anyone in a store knows the order exists

Fast Shop is one of Brazil's largest electronics retailers: 80+ stores, 22,000+ SKUs and around 2,500 orders a day. Its checkout sells delivery in two hours. That promise is made before a single person in a store has seen the order, and it is the reason Kruzer's platform was funded at all. Every order that misses it costs twice: once in the delivery, once in the customer who stops believing the badge next time.

Before Pick & Pack, the promise ran on people. A store found out about an order by phone. The distribution centre routed couriers from a spreadsheet. A driver got an address by message. When something stalled, whoever noticed first called whoever they thought was responsible, and nobody held a number for how often that ended on time. There is no on-time baseline to quote, and that is the point: nothing in the operation could produce one. It ran on WhatsApp threads, a courier app and spreadsheets. The absence of the number was the problem the project existed to solve.

So the number we set out to move was never “a better fulfilment UI.” It was the on-time rate, and underneath it the number that actually decides it: how long it takes an operator to find out an order is in trouble. Before the platform that was whenever an operator happened to notice, or whenever someone chased them about it. An operation only hits its SLA if the exceptions surface early enough to be fixed.

81% on time is a statement about the other 19% being findable.

02 · What the operation taught me

The stall list is the requirements list

Fast Shop's operations people had run this process for years without software. Treating them as stakeholders to be informed, rather than as the source of the requirements, would have produced a beautiful tool for a process that doesn't exist. So before drawing a screen I spent time with eight operators across three stores, asking two questions on the floor.

  1. Where does an order actually stop?

    Not in theory. The real stalls: waiting for a courier who arrived before the order was packed, a product not on the shelf where the system says it is, a customer who isn't home, an invoice rejected by the tax system. Each stall became a status, and each status became something an operator could act on instead of phone about.

  2. What happens when a delivery fails?

    The answer was uncomfortable and useful: nothing consistent. The order stayed “in transit” forever, the product came back to the store informally, and nobody was accountable. That gap is where the “returning to store” status came from later, and I only found it by asking about the ugly path instead of the happy one.

Two things could not change, and knowing them early kept the design from becoming an argument the operation would always win: the carrier integrations (Uber, Borzo and the store's own drivers) and the fiscal documents every order has to carry.

03 · Three roles, not a primary user

Same order, three people, three definitions of “done”

The store operator is hiring this to clear the queue before the courier arrives. The distribution-centre operator is hiring it to know which orders are about to breach and route around them. The driver is hiring it to get an unambiguous next stop. Most fulfilment tools pick one of those jobs and make the other two adapt.

Designing for three roles costs more than designing for one, and it had to be argued for before anyone had seen a screen. “Design it for the store operator and the rest will adapt” was a perfectly reasonable position. What won the argument was not an opinion but the map above: once the points where information fell between store, distribution centre and driver were visible on one page, nobody proposed picking a primary user again.

In the interface that decision shows up as one platform with a different home per role: a store sees its own queue, the distribution centre sees every store plus the numbers that say where the day is going wrong, and the driver gets the stop. Same order, same statuses, same colours, different first screen.

Holding all three at once is what forced every interface decision: what goes first in a card, what earns an alert, what is allowed to be one click.

04 · The first board was wrong

The round that rebuilt the board

You can't A/B test a warehouse, and I couldn't pull store operators off the floor for a lab session. So the round happened where the work is: at the counter, on their machine, with real orders in front of them, walking their actual day instead of a task I had written.

What we assumed

That the board should mirror the order's lifecycle, because that is how the system thinks about an order: received, invoiced, picked, dispatched, delivered.

What the round showed

Operators don't think in lifecycles. They think in what is about to hurt them: what leaves in the next hour, what is already late, what has nowhere to go. The first version made them translate.

So the board was rebuilt around their sequence rather than the system's, and the card was rebuilt around their first question. “Is this one late?” moved to the top-right corner in red. The remaining SLA moved onto the modality pill. The carrier logo moved to where the eye lands last, because it is the thing you need only after you have decided to act.

05 · The screens, annotated

The distribution centre: every store, and the numbers that say where the day is going wrong

The distribution centre sees all stores at once, so its first screen is not a queue but a set of counters: late, invoicing problems, transport problems, proof of delivery pending. Each one is a filter, not a chart. The job here is triage, and triage starts by clicking the number that hurts.

Allocation was the other thing this role needed that a store did not. Several operators work the same queue, so every card says who owns it, and a card allocated to someone else fades back instead of disappearing: you can still see it is being handled, you just can't grab it by mistake.

The same queue as a table, when the question changes

A board answers “what is the shape of my day.” It does not answer “which of these 104 orders has been sitting the longest,” and that is the question the distribution centre asks every hour. So the same queue toggles into a table: one row per order, sortable, with dwell time as its own column and a severity dot before the order number. Same data, same statuses, same colours, a different first question.

When an order becomes a phone call

When a customer calls, the person answering needs the whole story in seconds: how much of the SLA is left, where the order is, what has already been said. The order page puts the countdown first and in red when it is already breached, the history down the side in the order things happened, and a comment thread with attachments so that “the customer said to leave it with the neighbour” is on the order, not in someone's head.

The exceptions are the product

In logistics the happy path needs almost no design; it is the exceptions that decide whether an operator trusts the screen or works around it. These four states came out of the stall list and the rollout, not out of a design file. Each one replaces something that used to be a phone call.

06 · Rollout, store by store

The release wasn't a date. It was a sequence

Fulfilment software fails in production in ways it never fails in a demo, so the flagship store went first, alone, watched daily with its operators and managers until a full week passed without a new corner case. After that, waves of about twenty stores every two weeks. It stayed close enough to the team that a problem could be watched instead of reported. Each wave carried what the previous one had taught, and the full three months — rollout plus daily hypercare from our support team — was the experiment. After that it dropped to occasional tickets.

The exceptions did most of the teaching. A courier arriving before an order was packed. Orders scanned out of sequence. A delivery that failed with the address still marked valid. Each became either an interface state or a rule, and I stayed in engineering's reviews through every wave, because the difference between an operator trusting the screen and working around it is usually one unhandled case.

“Returning to store” is the one I would point to. It did not exist in the first version. The first wave surfaced orders sitting in “in transit” with nowhere to go. The operators had a workaround for it but no name, which is why it had never been requested.

07 · What it changed, measured

The rate did not go up. It refused to go down while the load went up

Three months after rollout, on-time delivery against the two-hour promise was 81%, on roughly 38% more volume than the operation had ever handled. There is no before to hold it against, because the operation had never measured itself — so read 81% as the first honest figure this operation ever had about itself, not as an improvement over a known number. That is the whole claim, and it is worth stating carefully: in fulfilment, holding a rate while volume grows is the harder of the two outcomes.

How it was measured

  • Headline On-time rate against the two-hour promise, the platform's own figure, read three months after rollout. Baseline: none. The pre-platform operation produced no on-time figure, because it ran on WhatsApp, a courier app and spreadsheets. I present 81% as a first measurement, not as a delta.
  • Operational Where orders sat and for how long, by status and by store. The platform measures this now; nothing measured it before, so there is no before-and-after to give. A rate says there is a problem; this says where to go.
  • Adoption Whether operators worked in the platform or around it. We never counted spreadsheet exports before and after, so I can only report what the team saw: the parallel spreadsheets stopped being maintained. A blunt signal, and an uncounted one.
  • Not proven Causality. The platform launched alongside a growing operation, and I cannot separate the design from the process changes Fast Shop made in the same months. The staged rollout is the closest thing to a control: stores on the platform versus stores not yet on it, over the same weeks.

Scope, for context, not as outcome: 80+ stores migrated onto the platform, 22,000+ SKUs flowing through it, three products delivered on the same foundation (Pick & Pack, PIM, DevTools), all designed by one person with Compass as the system underneath. On precision: the figures above are what I and the people who owned the numbers recall, not a report I can still pull — I left the company and the dashboards with it. Treat the magnitudes as approximate and the direction as the claim. Where a number never existed, this page says so rather than reconstructing one.

08 · What I'd do differently

I designed the happy path first. In logistics, the exceptions are the product

I would have gone to the floor before drawing the first board, not after it fell short. The rework was cheap because it happened early, but it was avoidable: I built the first version from the system's model of an order because that was the model I had access to, and I mistook availability for accuracy. The wider version of that mistake is that I treated exceptions as a second pass, when three of the four states above are the reason operators use the tool at all.

I would also have instrumented time-to-detect from day one. It was the pointer we agreed on, and for the first wave I could only read it off the stall list and the dwell times, not off a single number. The releases after that had it; the one people ask about most did not.

My role

The only designer on the platform, Sep 2024 to Feb 2026. I owned Pick & Pack from discovery through the store-by-store rollout, built and governed Compass, and designed PIM and DevTools on the same foundation. With no other designer to hand anything to, prioritising between the four products was part of the job: Pick & Pack first, because it carried the two-hour promise.

The team, and what was mine

A product manager owned the roadmap and the OKRs with Fast Shop; Fast Shop's operations leads were the domain experts and the source of the stall list; one engineering team per product built it, with me in their reviews through every wave. Decisions that were mine: three roles instead of a primary user, the rebuilt board and card, the table toggle, the four exception states above, and “returning to store.”

Compass: the system underneath

None of these screens were designed in isolation. A status pill means the same thing on the board, the table and the order page because the token layer says so. That work grew large enough to be its own case.
