Pick & Pack: one order, three people, one screen each

Fast Shop sells two-hour delivery, and the process behind that promise ran on phone calls and spreadsheets. I designed the platform that replaced them for the store, the distribution centre and the driver at once, with the exceptions treated as first-class screens.

01 · Why it was hard

Two hours, promised before anyone in a store knows the order exists

Fast Shop runs 80+ stores, 22,000+ SKUs and around 2,500 orders a day. Checkout sells delivery in two hours before anyone in a store has seen the order, and before Pick & Pack that promise ran on phone calls, a courier app and spreadsheets. The operation couldn’t even produce an on-time figure.

The number worth moving was time-to-detect: how long it takes an operator to find out an order is in trouble. Before the platform, that was whenever someone happened to notice or chased them about it, and an SLA is only met if exceptions surface early enough to fix.

I spent time with store operators asking where an order actually stops and what happens when a delivery fails. The honest answer was a manual loop: cancel the ride in the courier app, request a new one, track the driver by hand. Two things couldn’t change: the carrier integrations and the fiscal documents every order carries.

02 · Decision: three roles, not one primary user
1

One platform, three homes, one order

Problem

“Design it for the store operator and the rest will adapt” was a perfectly reasonable default. Most fulfilment tools pick one job and make the other two adapt to it.

Decision

Design for three jobs at once. A store sees its own queue, the distribution centre sees every store plus where the day is going wrong, the driver gets one unambiguous stop. Same order, same statuses, same colours, different first screen.

Why

The store needs to clear the queue before the courier arrives, the distribution centre needs to route around what’s about to breach, and the driver needs one clear next stop. Holding all three decided what goes first on a card and what earns an alert.

Result

One platform shipped with a different home per role, and no store, distribution centre or driver had to translate someone else's workflow into their own.

03 · The store board

The store board, shaped by the first delivery round

After the first round of deliveries, store operators came back with two requests. “I need to see the invoice number on the card, fast, to move my process along”: the fiscal invoice was what they looked up most, so it moved onto the card. “I need the most-late orders at the top, shouting on the screen, so I know what to prioritise”: lateness became the sort order and the loudest thing on the card, with time in status in red at the top, the remaining SLA on the pill and the carrier logo last.

04 · What shipped: the screens

What shipped: the screens

The distribution centre: every store, and the numbers that say where the day is going wrong

The distribution centre sees every store at once, so its first screen is four counters (late, invoicing, transport, proof pending), each one a filter. Several operators work the same queue here, so every card shows who owns it.

The same queue as a table, when the question changes

Every hour the distribution centre asks which of 104 orders has waited longest, a question a board can’t answer. So the same queue toggles into a sortable table, with time in queue as its own column.

When an order becomes a phone call

Whoever answers a customer call needs the whole story in seconds: the SLA countdown first, the history in order, and comments with attachments on the order itself instead of in someone’s head.

The exceptions are the product

In logistics, the exceptions decide whether an operator trusts the screen or works around it. Each of these four replaced something that used to be a phone call.

05 · Rollout, store by store

The release wasn't a date. It was a sequence

The flagship store went first, alone, watched daily until a full week passed without a new corner case. Then waves of about twenty stores every two weeks, close enough that problems were watched instead of reported. Three months of rollout and daily hypercare, then occasional tickets.

Each wave surfaced cases the first version didn’t handle, and each became a state or a rule. “Returning to store” is the one I’d point to. When a driver couldn’t deliver, the operator had to cancel the ride in the Uber app, request a new one to bring the package back and track the driver in the app by hand. We built that return into the platform: when a delivery fails, the trip back is created automatically and the operator follows it on the panel.

06 · Impact

The operation didn’t slow down. Even as the load went up

Three months after rollout, the platform was running the two-hour promise across 80+ stores on more volume than the operation had ever handled. Operators stopped keeping parallel spreadsheets, and a failed delivery went from invisible to a status with a next step.

How it was measured: the operation never produced an on-time figure before the platform, so there is no verified baseline, and I don’t present an estimate as one. Causality isn’t proven either: Fast Shop changed processes in the same months. The staged rollout, stores on the platform next to stores not yet on it, is the closest thing to a control.

07 · What I'd do differently

I shipped the first card without asking what operators look up first

The two changes that made the store board work, the invoice number on the card and the most-late orders on top, came from operators after the first delivery round. An afternoon watching a store counter before shipping would have surfaced both. I would also have captured time-to-detect from the first release, so this page could show a measured before-and-after.

My role

The only designer on the platform, Sep 2024 to Feb 2026, across Pick & Pack, Compass, PIM and DevTools. Pick & Pack came first because it carried the two-hour promise.

The team, and what was mine

A product manager owned the roadmap and OKRs with Fast Shop, Fast Shop’s operations leads were the domain experts, and one engineering team per product built it with me through every wave. Mine: three roles instead of a primary user, the store board and card, the table toggle, the four exception states and “returning to store.”

Compass: the system underneath

A status pill means the same thing on the board, the table and the order page because the token layer underneath says so. That work became its own case.
