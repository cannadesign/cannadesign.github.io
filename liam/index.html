LIAM: turning up to 2 hours of clinical paperwork into a 25-minute review

Therapists at Genial Care were losing up to two hours after every session reconstructing it from memory for mandatory clinical reports. LIAM lets them narrate instead, and hands back a draft they review and sign.

Every session at Genial Care generates a clinical record: what was targeted, what the child did, what changed. Therapists were writing that record from memory, after the fact, on top of a full day of sessions, which is exactly the kind of task that gets rushed, delayed, or thinned out under pressure. LIAM's job was to move the record-keeping into the moment it actually happens, instead of the moment there's finally time for it.

01 · Why it was hard

Two hours of reconstruction, and the constraint underneath it

The paperwork happened at the wrong time. A therapist finishes a session, moves to the next child, and writes the record hours later from memory, losing the specific detail from minute twelve that the record exists to capture, on top of a full day of sessions.

The number worth moving was documentation time per session, because of what sits on the other side of it: a therapist’s day has a fixed number of hours, and every hour spent reconstructing a session is an hour not spent in one. That caps how many sessions a therapist can carry, which caps how fast the company can grow the care it delivers. So documentation load limits capacity. It is also a retention risk, since unpaid evening paperwork wears clinicians down over time.

An AI feature in a clinical context fails on governance long before it fails on usability. Before I designed a screen, the team had to agree who signs the record, what the clinical record needed to contain, and how long a session recording is retained. Faster notes that said less would have moved the metric and damaged the product, so the clinical lead held a veto: a time saving bought with thinner records counted as a failure, not a win.

02 · Decision: LIAM proposes, the clinician decides
1

LIAM proposes. The clinician decides.

Problem

If LIAM drafts and submits on its own, authorship blurs, and a wrong clinical record with no clear author is a liability, not a convenience.

Decision

LIAM drafts every required field as a suggestion. A therapist reads, corrects, and submits. Nothing reaches the record unread.

Why

The real design question wasn’t whether it could draft well, it was what happens the first time it’s confidently wrong. That produced four interface rules: every field is a suggestion copied in by hand, never pre-submitted; the source recording stays attached so a therapist can check a line against what was actually said; nothing reaches the record without a person reading it; and an in-flow prompt asks whether the suggestions are still good, so quality drift shows up as a falling answer rate instead of a habit that quietly dies.

What it caught

Before touching the recording flow, we mapped the existing clinical panel section by section on a wall, not to redesign it, but to argue concretely about where a voice note belongs inside a workspace therapists already use, instead of assuming it needed a place of its own.

Result

The interaction model held through launch: LIAM proposes, the clinician decides, and record quality stayed the clinical lead’s to veto.

03 · Decision: how I read trust
2

Put the trust signal inside the flow

Problem

On the family app, behavior showed up in a weekly funnel. Here the population is the ~80 therapists on staff, the event happens a handful of times a day, and the real risk (a therapist quietly losing trust in a draft) never shows up in a funnel at all.

Decision

Put a lightweight prompt in the flow itself, asking whether LIAM’s suggestions are still good, so suggestion quality could be read from real usage after launch.

Why

What a therapist needs from an AI here is to keep what just happened while it’s still accurate. That made trust the thing actually at risk, more than writing quality, and a funnel can’t see a therapist who quietly stops trusting a draft but keeps clicking.

What it caught

Therapists tested the first version and the response was positive, with one consistent problem: LIAM transcribed the narration literally, so some passages read as fragments that didn’t make sense in a clinical record. We added a step that restructures the narration into cohesive sentences that fit the context of the session before it reaches the form, so therapists stopped rewriting the wording by hand.

Result

The prompt shipped with LIAM. I left Genial Care before there was a quarter of data to read, so I don’t claim what it showed.

04 · What shipped

What shipped

Three pieces carried the two decisions above into a working product.

Recording, inside the work already happening

The intervention screen, with its programs, targets, and progress per skill, is where a therapist already spends the session. A single mic control sits in the corner of that exact screen, not in a separate “documentation” tool. Only the therapist is recorded: they narrate what they are doing and how the child responds. The child is never the input. Once recorded, the note surfaces as a waveform with an explicit send action, so the therapist can confirm the capture before moving on. And when an upload fails (clinics have bad wifi), the interface says so directly, “your voice note wasn’t sent,” with an obvious retry, instead of a spinner that quietly gives up.

The draft: a form the therapist edits, not writes

This is what the two hours collapse into. LIAM turns the audio into a first draft of every required field (health check-in, reinforcers used, challenges observed, session summary, clinical discussion points) each pre-filled and clearly marked as a suggestion, with a one-click copy into the real field. The therapist’s job changes from writing the report to editing one: read, correct what’s off, keep what’s right, submit. Nothing submits itself; the “Save” action stays with the therapist.

A feedback loop that keeps trust visible

An AI feature that quietly gets worse, or quietly gets better, teaches people to stop trusting it either way. A lightweight prompt sits directly in the flow: are you happy with LIAM’s suggestions right now, yes or no, with room to say why, so drift in suggestion quality surfaces from real usage instead of a support ticket or a churned habit months later. The decision underneath all three pieces is the same: an AI feature earns trust through visible mechanics, not through confidence.

05 · Impact

Impact

Up to 2h
Writing the record from memory after a full day, per session, before LIAM (self-reported)
~25min
Reviewing and submitting LIAM's draft, per session, after
~80
Therapists on the clinical team: the before-and-after covers all of them, not a sample

How it was measured, and what the number leaves out: the two hours are the therapists’ own self-reported time to write a session report from memory before LIAM; the 25 minutes are their review-and-submit time with LIAM’s draft in place, read by the team one quarter after rollout. I had left Genial Care by then: I designed and shipped LIAM, and the 25-minute figure is what the clinical team reported back. It is not a like-for-like total: narrating during the session adds a few minutes that the 25 does not include, and the population is the clinical team on staff, so this is a before-and-after with everyone, not a sample. The clinical lead’s verdict after launch was that record quality improved rather than merely held: a record written minutes after the session carries detail that one written from memory at 9pm does not.

The pressure on an AI feature is always to make it do more. The constraint I held was that LIAM proposes and never submits, even when the draft is good enough that signing it unread would be tempting. It cost us the most impressive version of the demo. It’s the reason the clinical side allowed the feature to exist at all.

06 · What I’d do differently

What I’d do differently

I would have brought the clinical lead into the interface decisions, not only the review of the output: several trust states would have been simpler if the person who owns the clinical standard had been in the room while they were being drawn. And I under-designed the moment a therapist disagrees with the draft: correcting is easy, but the interface never asks why, so the most valuable signal in the whole feature goes unrecorded.

Where this sits in the bigger picture

LIAM shipped after the family-facing app had already gone from 8% to 62% daily use. Once families were actually engaging, the constraint moved to the other side of the platform: the therapists producing the content families were now relying on. Same instinct, different audience: find where a person is spending effort on something that isn't the actual work, and compress it into a decision they can make quickly.
