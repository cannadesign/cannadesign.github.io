LIAM: turning 2 hours of clinical paperwork into a 25-minute review

Therapists at Genial Care were losing up to two hours after every session reconstructing it from memory for mandatory clinical reports. LIAM lets them narrate instead, and hands back a draft to review, not a form to fill.

Every session at Genial Care generates a clinical record: what was targeted, what the child did, what changed. Therapists were writing that record from memory, after the fact, on top of a full day of sessions, which is exactly the kind of task that gets rushed, delayed, or thinned out under pressure. LIAM's job was to move the record-keeping into the moment it actually happens, instead of the moment there's finally time for it.

01 · The pain

Two hours of reconstruction, on top of a full day of sessions

The paperwork wasn’t hard, it was misplaced. A therapist finishes a session, moves to the next child, and writes the first record hours later from memory. What gets lost isn’t effort, it’s detail: the specific thing the child did at minute twelve, which is exactly the detail the record exists to capture.

Everyone already knew documentation was heavy. What the team didn’t have was a shared account of where the two hours went, which is a different question and the only one a design can act on.

02 · The pointer

The pointer: hours per therapist, and what those hours were costing

The number we set out to move was documentation time per session. It matters because of what sits on the other side of it: a therapist’s day has a fixed number of hours, and every hour spent reconstructing a session is an hour not spent in one. Documentation load is a capacity constraint wearing an administrative costume, and it is also a retention risk. Unpaid evening paperwork is a reliable way to lose good clinicians.

There was a second pointer nobody writes down: record quality. Faster notes that say less would have moved the metric and damaged the product. So we agreed the clinical lead held a veto, and that a time saving bought with thinner records counted as a failure, not a win.

A time saving bought with thinner clinical records counted as a failure, not a win.

03 · The questions

What I had to settle with stakeholders before designing anything

An AI feature in a clinical context fails on governance long before it fails on usability. So the first round wasn’t about screens.

  1. Who signs the record?

    If LIAM drafts and a therapist submits, the therapist is still the author. That single answer set the whole interaction model: LIAM proposes, a human disposes, and nothing reaches the record without a person having read it.

  2. What must a report contain, non-negotiably?

    The clinical team defined the fields that exist for regulatory and continuity-of-care reasons. Those became constraints on the output, not suggestions, and they are why the draft is structured rather than free prose.

  3. Where does a child’s voice recording live, and for how long?

    Audio of a session is sensitive data about a minor. Retention, access and deletion had to be answered before I designed a record button, because the answers change what the interface has to promise.

  4. What happens the first time it is confidently wrong?

    Not if. I made the team answer for the case where a plausible-sounding draft contains something the session didn’t contain, because the answer to that question is the difference between an assistant and a liability. The answer became four interface rules: every field is marked as a suggestion and copied in by hand, never pre-submitted; the source recording stays attached to the draft so a therapist can check a line against what was actually said; nothing reaches the record without a person having read it; and the in-flow prompt asks whether the suggestions are still good, so a drift in quality shows up as a falling answer rate.

Part of that round happened on a wall. Before deciding anything about the recording flow, we mapped the clinical panel as it already existed — every section, every tab, everything a therapist passes through in a session. The point was not to redesign the panel. It was to be able to point at a specific place and ask whether an audio note belonged there, which is a much harder question to dodge than “where should this feature live?”

“What happens the first time it is confidently wrong” is the question that separates an assistant from a liability.

04 · The job

What a therapist is hiring this for

Not “write my report.” A therapist is hiring LIAM to not lose what just happened. To get the session out of their head while it is still accurate, and to get their evening back without lowering the standard of the record they sign.

That framing rules things out. It means the capture has to happen next to the child, in the minute after the session, on the device already in their hand. A better form, anywhere else, doesn’t do the job.

A therapist isn’t hiring an AI to write. They’re hiring it to not lose what just happened.

05 · The bet

Designing an AI feature people can trust

The hard part of LIAM was never the model. It was earning the therapist's trust in something that drafts clinical language on their behalf. So the process was built around validating trust as much as usability.

06 · The test

How I checked, with a population you can count and no funnel

On the family app, a few hundred metres away in the same company, I read behaviour off a weekly curve. Here that instrument is useless. The population is the ~80 therapists on staff, the event happens a handful of times a day, and the thing at risk never appears in a funnel: whether a clinician trusts a draft enough to sign it. A therapist who quietly stops trusting LIAM still clicks every button.

So the evidence had to be qualitative and close: sitting with the documentation as it actually happens, testing the risky states rather than the demo path, and reading AI-drafted output line by line with the people who would have to sign it. Small n, but the right n. When you can count everyone who will use it, you are not sampling. You are asking all of them.

The quantitative part came after launch, and by design. An in-flow prompt asks whether the suggestion was still good, so that drift shows up as a falling answer rate rather than as a habit that quietly died months later. It has stayed around 70% positive, on a team that answers product prompts readily — these therapists turn up for interviews and tests when asked, which makes the signal easier to read here than it would be on a colder population. About 74% of sessions now go through LIAM. Both figures are internal readings I recall rather than a report I can produce, and I flag them as such.

When you can count everyone who will use it, that isn’t a sample. That is everybody.

My role

Senior product designer end to end: workflow research, interaction and UI, prototype, and the trust-and-safety states that make an AI feature usable in a clinical context.

The team

Partnered with a product manager, an engineering squad building the AI pipeline, and the clinical team as the domain authority on what a report must contain.

Recording lives inside the work, not next to it

The intervention screen, with its programs, targets, and progress per skill, is where a therapist already spends the session. Adding LIAM meant putting the record button there, not in a separate "documentation" tool the therapist would have to remember to open afterward. A single mic control sits in the corner of the exact screen they're already using.

Confirm the capture before asking the therapist to trust it

Once recorded, the note doesn't vanish into a black box. It surfaces as a waveform with its length, and an explicit send action. That pause matters more than it looks: a therapist just spoke clinical information out loud about a child, and the interface's next job is to prove it actually captured that before asking them to move on.

Uploads fail. The interface has to say so, plainly

A voice note recorded in a clinic doesn't always make it to the server on the first try. I designed the failure state as a direct, specific statement, "your voice note wasn't sent," with an obvious retry, instead of a spinner that quietly gives up or an error code the therapist has to interpret. For a tool handling clinical documentation, silent failure is worse than an ugly one.

Silent failure is worse than an ugly one, especially in a tool handling clinical documentation.

07 · What the evidence changed

The payoff: a draft, not a form

This is what the two hours collapse into. LIAM turns the audio into a first draft of every required field: health check-in, reinforcers used, challenges observed, session summary, clinical discussion points, each one pre-filled and clearly marked as LIAM's suggestion, with a one-click copy into the real field. The therapist's job changes from writing the report to editing one: read, correct what's off, keep what's right, submit.

Nothing here submits itself. Every field stays editable, and the "Save" action stays with the therapist: LIAM proposes, the clinician decides.

08 · The build

Shipping it with the AI squad, not next to them

An AI feature has failure modes that don’t exist in ordinary product work, and most of them only appear against the real pipeline. I worked inside the squad building it. That is where latency stopped being an abstraction. A therapist standing next to a child will not wait on a spinner, so the interface had to be honest about what was still processing, and let her leave the screen without losing the note.

The upload failure state came out of that phase, not out of a design file. Clinics have bad wifi, and the first implementation retried quietly and then gave up. We replaced it with a plain statement and a retry the therapist controls. I stayed on the review side after release too, reading drafts against the fields the clinical team had defined, because a model that drifts doesn’t announce it.

A model that drifts doesn’t announce it. Someone has to keep reading the output.

09 · Into production

Trust isn't a one-time decision. It's a loop

An AI feature that quietly gets worse, or quietly gets better, teaches people to stop trusting it either way. I added a lightweight feedback prompt directly in the flow: are you happy with LIAM's suggestions right now, yes or no, with room to say why, so drift in suggestion quality surfaces from real usage instead of waiting for a support ticket or a churned habit.

What I'd defend in a design review

The design decision underneath all five screens is the same: an AI feature earns trust through visible mechanics, not through confidence. Show the capture before asking for the record. State the failure instead of hiding it. Mark every suggestion as a suggestion.

How it was measured, and what the number leaves out: the two hours are the therapists’ own self-reported time to write a session report from memory before LIAM; the 25 minutes are their review-and-submit time with LIAM’s draft in place. It is not a like-for-like total: narrating during the session adds a few minutes that the 25 does not include, and the population is the clinical team on staff, so this is a before-and-after with everyone, not a sample. Record quality was the guardrail and the clinical lead held the veto on it. On precision: the figures above are what I and the people who owned the numbers recall, not a report I can still pull — I left the company and the dashboards with it. Treat the magnitudes as approximate and the direction as the claim. Where a number never existed, this page says so rather than reconstructing one.

How we measured it, and the number that could veto the win

Time was the headline, but it was never allowed to be the only number.

  • Headline Documentation time per session, read against the therapists’ own before-and-after rather than against an estimate.
  • Guardrail Record quality, reviewed by the clinical lead. A faster note that said less counted as a failure, and the clinical side held the veto on that call. Their verdict after launch was that quality improved rather than held: a record written minutes after the session, from the session, carries detail that a record written from memory at 9pm does not.
  • Drift alarm The in-flow prompt asking whether LIAM’s suggestions are still good, so decay shows up as a falling answer rate instead of as a support ticket months later.

That was also the argument I had to win. The pressure on an AI feature is always to make it do more, and the constraint I held was that LIAM proposes and never submits — the therapist stays the author, even when the draft is good enough that signing it unread would be tempting. It cost us the most impressive version of the demo. It is the reason the clinical side allowed the feature to exist at all.

What I’d do differently

I would have brought the clinical lead into the interface decisions, not only into the review of the output. The trust states were designed first and validated second; several of them would have been simpler if the person who owns the clinical standard had been in the room while they were being drawn. And I under-designed the moment a therapist disagrees with the draft: correcting is easy, but the interface never asks why, so the most valuable signal in the whole feature goes unrecorded.

Where this sits in the bigger picture

LIAM shipped after the family-facing app had already gone from 8% to 62% daily use. Once families were actually engaging, the constraint moved to the other side of the platform: the therapists producing the content families were now relying on. Same instinct, different audience: find where a person is spending effort on something that isn't the actual work, and compress it into a decision they can make quickly.
